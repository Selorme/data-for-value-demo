An assistant that tells once and shares only what you choose

We are four students asking whether personal AI agents make everyday services easier for people with lower digital confidence, and whether people can understand and control what the agent does for them. This page follows the project from the first brief to our first workshop, and what comes next.

      The eight invented people

      Same trip, different lives

      Every person has around 8,000 rows of made-up records across health, benefits, bank, location, calendar, shopping and travel. None of it belongs to anyone real. Each trip uses a different way to travel and meets the same kinds of place: transport, a hotel, its bistro, an event and an insurer.

      What we learned

      Four findings, three open questions

      Finding 1

      Knowing the technology did not mean saying yes

      The most tech-confident group we could find kept most data back. What they shared depended on the place: the event and hotel got most of what they asked for, the insurer almost nothing.

      Finding 2

      Trust depended on the conditions of sharing

      People asked for extra checks before transactions that matter, security they could believe, clear retention, and care with chats and photos because other people are in them.

      Finding 3

      Needing access weakens the freedom to say no

      People with disabilities can be pushed to disclose more to get a service that works. Sharing a need or a verified disability card was preferred to sharing a diagnosis.

      Finding 4

      Understanding the controls is part of control

      People wanted a short walkthrough, finer choices (a degree without all the grades), the reason for each request, and a way to change their mind, knowing copies already sent can’t be recalled.

      Risk understanding or real preference?

      Probably both. One participant preferred to share nothing at all; two were open to it if the risks were reduced. Age may play a part.

      When would safeguards change a decision?

      Which protections would people believe, and how do we show them in a way that is honest and understandable?

      How much benefit would it take?

      Everything we proposed lowers the risk. We never asked how big the benefit must be before people say yes.

      What this round can’t tell us

      • Four technologically confident students are not older adults or people with lived access needs.
      • Nobody had grown up in the EU; all came from countries with weaker data protection laws.
      • They decided for an invented person, so the benefits weren’t theirs, but their privacy instincts were.
      • Time, breach and insurance numbers come from the simulation we wrote.

      Next

      What we change, and what we test next

      A first-use walkthrough

      Built around a real task: what Helmi reads, what a business receives, and when you are asked.

      Finer, reversible permissions

      Split bundled items, show purpose and duration, and keep reading, sharing and deciding separate.

      Share the need, not the record

      Short answers like “needs step-free access” that people can preview and correct before they go.

      Autonomy in steps

      Suggestions, drafts and limited rules, human checks for big actions, and only security claims we can back up.

      RQ1

      What evidence changes a sharing decision? Including the size of the benefit and safeguards people can see.

      RQ2

      Can agents lower the privacy cost of accessibility? A need or eligibility proof instead of a diagnosis.

      RQ3

      What makes consent usable across digital confidence? Do walkthroughs and permission histories help, or add work?

      RQ4

      Which decisions should Helmi make alone? By the stakes, the recipient and how sensitive the data is.

      Workshop 2 keeps the hotel, adds a health or disability scenario, and compares sharing the raw record, an editable summary and declining, with participants who have lower digital confidence, lived access needs, or grew up in the EU.
